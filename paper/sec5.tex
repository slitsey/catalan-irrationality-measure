\section{The real place}\label{sec:real}

In this section we prove that $\Delta_N$ is small not only at $G$ but on an exponentially small
interval around it.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:real}
Put $\rho=259299/2500$ and $\eta_\infty=5993/3125$. For all large $N$,
\[
 |Q_N(x)|\le e^{-\eta_\infty N^2}\qquad\text{whenever }|x-G|\le e^{-\rho N}.
\]
\end{theorem}

The proof has three parts. We first reduce the perturbation $\Delta_N(x)-\Delta_N(G)$ to
determinants in which some columns of $\cA_N$ are replaced by values of the row functions at fixed
points (\S\ref{ssec:quadrature}--\ref{ssec:andreief}). We then bound such determinants by OpenAI's
energy method, extended to configurations with fewer free points (\S\ref{ssec:energy}). Finally we
supply the trial sequences that the energy method needs and verify the resulting inequalities
(\S\ref{ssec:trials}--\ref{ssec:cells}).

\subsection{A quadrature formula for the coefficient of the constant}\label{ssec:quadrature}
Let $\delta=x-G$. Then $\Delta_N(x)=\det(\cA_N+\delta\cL_N)$, and the question is how large the
columns of $\cL_N$ are compared with those of $\cA_N$. By \eqref{eq:L} the entries of $\cL_N$ are linear
functionals of the coefficients of the row polynomials. Since these polynomials have bounded degree,
the functionals can be written exactly as finite combinations of point evaluations: if
$\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_J$ are distinct points and $J$ exceeds the degree of the polynomials involved,
Lagrange interpolation gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:quad}
 \cL_N(r,k)=\sum_{a=1}^JA_r(\tau_a)\,\beta_{a,k},
\end{equation}
where the weights $\beta_{a,k}$ are the values of the same functionals on the Lagrange basis
polynomials. This is a Gauss-type quadrature formula, exact for polynomials of the relevant degree.
The point of the formula is that the columns of $\cL_N$ become combinations of point evaluations of the
same row functions that define $\cA_N$, so that the perturbed determinant can again be treated by the
energy method. The cost is the size of the weights.

We take $J=18N$ and the nodes
\[
 t_{j,\pm}=\pm\sqrt{a^2+(1-a^2)(1+s_j)/2},\qquad T_{9N}(s_j)=0,\qquad
 a=\frac{2g_0}{1+g_0^2},\quad g_0=\frac{401}{2500},
\]
that is, the roots of $P_N(t)=T_{9N}\bigl((2t^2-(1+a^2))/(1-a^2)\bigr)$. They lie in
$(-1,-a)\cup(a,1)$. In the coordinate $x$ with $t=2x/(1+x^2)$ every node has $|x|>g_0$; this matters
below, because the energy fields are larger near $x=0$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:quadrature}
After removal of the common factor of the rows, the weights in \eqref{eq:quad} satisfy
\[
 |\beta_{a,k}|\le108000N\,e^{17.913N}.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
At a root, the derivative identity for Chebyshev polynomials gives $|P_N'(t_{j,\pm})|\ge36Na/(1-a^2)$.
The weight is a coefficient functional of the Lagrange polynomial, and every coefficient of a
polynomial is bounded by its maximum modulus on a circle centered at the origin. On the circle
$|z|=r$ with $r=1001/1000$, write $s=|\operatorname{Re}z|$. For $w=(2z^2-(1+a^2))/(1-a^2)$ one has
$|T_{9N}(w)|\le(\Theta+\sqrt{\Theta^2-1})^{9N}$, where
\[
 \Theta(s)=\frac{\sqrt{(r^2+1)^2-4s^2}+\sqrt{(r^2+a^2)^2-4a^2s^2}}{1-a^2}
\]
is half the sum of the distances from $w$ to $\pm1$. The function $\Theta$ decreases in $s$, while the factor
$|1-z|^2\le r^2+1+2s$ coming from the column filter increases. A cover of $[0,r]$ by $55$ intervals,
with rational upper bounds for the square roots and logarithms at their endpoints, proves
\[
 9\log\bigl(\Theta(s)+\sqrt{\Theta(s)^2-1}\bigr)+\log(r^2+1+2s)<17.913\qquad(0\le s\le r).
\]
Since $|z-t_{j,\pm}|\ge1/1000$ on the circle and $(1-a^2)/(36a)\le1$, the bound follows.
\end{proof}

The circle of radius $1.001$ instead of $1$ separates the circle from the nodes; on the unit circle itself
the cancellation in the factor $(1-t)^N$ near $t=1$ is what keeps the bound small.

\subsection{Column expansion and an identity of Andr\'eief type}\label{ssec:andreief}
Expanding $\det(\cA_N+\delta\Phi\beta)$, where $\Phi(r,a)=A_r(\tau_a)$, multilinearly in the columns gives
a sum over maps $f$ from the columns to $\{\varnothing,1,\ldots,J\}$ of
$\prod_{f(k)\ne\varnothing}\delta\beta_{f(k),k}\det M_f$. Here $M_f$ is the matrix whose $k$-th column is the
$k$-th column of $\cA_N$ if $f(k)=\varnothing$ and the vector $(A_r(\tau_{f(k)}))_r$ otherwise.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:expansion}
If $|\beta_{a,k}|\le B$ and $|\det M_f|\le Mc^{m(f)}$ for every $f$, where $m(f)$ is the number of columns
replaced, then
\[
 \bigl|\det(\cA_N+\delta\Phi\beta)-\det\cA_N\bigr|\le M\bigl((1+J|\delta|Bc)^{n}-1\bigr).
\]
\end{lemma}

Each $M_f$ has $p=n-m$ columns that are integrals and $m$ columns that are point values. Andr\'eief's
identity extends to this situation.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:andreief}
Let $\mu,\nu$ be finite measures, $F_r$ $(r<p+m)$, $G_i$ $(i<p)$ and $K$ integrable functions, and let $B$
be the matrix with $B(r,i)=\iint F_r(t)K(t,s)G_i(s)\,d\mu(t)\,d\nu(s)$ for $i<p$ and
$B(r,p+j)=F_r(\tau'_j)$. Then
\[
 (p!)^2\det B=\int\!\!\int\det\bigl[F_r((t\Vert\tau')_c)\bigr]_{r,c}
 \det[K(t_i,s_j)]_{i,j<p}\det[G_i(s_j)]_{j,i<p}\,d\mu^p(t)\,d\nu^p(s),
\]
where $t\Vert\tau'$ is the list of the $p$ variables followed by the $m$ fixed points.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For $m=0$ this is Andr\'eief's identity applied twice \cite{Forrester2018Andreief}. In general expand
$\det B$ multilinearly in its first $p$ columns, which introduces one pair of variables $(t_i,s_i)$ for
each of them, and antisymmetrize first in the $t$ and then in the $s$ variables; the last $m$ columns are
constant throughout.
\end{proof}

With $K(t,s)=1/(1-ts)$ the middle determinant is the Cauchy determinant, and the third is a
generalized Vandermonde determinant $\prod_js_j^b(1-s_j)^q\det[s_j^{E_i}]$. For the latter we use the
bound
\begin{equation}\label{eq:genvand}
 \bigl|\det[s_j^{E_i}]\bigr|\le\Bigl(\prod_j(1+s_j)\Bigr)^{n-p}\prod_{i<j}|s_i-s_j|,
\end{equation}
valid for distinct exponents $E_i<n$ and $s_j\in[0,1]$. It follows by multiplying the matrix by a
unitriangular matrix built from the polynomial $\prod_j(X-s_j)$ and bounding the remaining $(n-p)\times(n-p)$
determinant by the product of the $\ell^1$ norms of its columns. The quotient
$\det[s_j^{E_i}]/\prod_{i<j}(s_j-s_i)$ is a Schur polynomial, so the bound is close to optimal.

\subsection{The energy estimate with fixed points}\label{ssec:energy}
OpenAI bounds the integrand in Andr\'eief's identity by the exponential of $n^2E$, where $E$ is a discrete
logarithmic energy of the points $x_i$ and $s_j$ in the coordinate $t=2x/(1+x^2)$. The energy consists of
an external field for each point, logarithmic interactions $\log|x_i-x_j|$, $\log(1-x_ix_j)$ and
$\log|s_i-s_j|$ among points of the same kind, and an interaction $-\log(1-2x_is_j+x_i^2)$ between the two
kinds. There are two cases, according to which of OpenAI's two bounds for the determinant of the row
functions is used \cite[Props.~5.2, 5.3]{OpenAI2026}; in the second case the interaction among the $x_i$
has weight $2$ instead of $1$.

The energy is bounded from above by duality. Given trial sequences $u=(u_k)$ and $v=(v_k)$, one writes
the interaction terms as quadratic forms in the moments $\frac1n\sum_iT_k(x_i)$ and
$\frac1n\sum_js_j^k$ and completes the squares against $u$ and $v$. What remains is the sum of the norms
$\|u\|^2$, $\|v\|^2$ and, for each point, a function of one variable,
\[
 X(x)=W_x(x)-2\kappa\,T(u,x)-S(v,x),\qquad Y(s)=W_s(s)+2\,T(v,s),
\]
where $W_x,W_s$ are the external fields, $\kappa\in\{1,2\}$ is the case, and $T(u,x)=\sum_ku_kT_k(x)/k$,
$S(v,x)=\sum_kv_kx^k/k$ are the potentials of the trial sequences. If $X\le M_X$ and $Y\le M_Y$ on the whole
domains, the energy of any configuration is at most a constant determined by $M_X$, $M_Y$ and the norms.
In potential-theoretic terms the extremal configuration is described by an equilibrium measure for two
interacting measures in an external field \cite{SaffTotik}, and the best trial sequences are its moments,
as Carreira observed \cite{Carreira2026}; any trial sequences give a valid upper bound.

When $m$ of the $n$ columns are point values, the configuration has only $p=n-m$ free points of each
kind, while the normalization is still by $n$. The fixed points contribute through the determinant of the
row functions evaluated at the concatenated list $t\Vert\tau'$. One checks that every step of OpenAI's
argument remains valid uniformly in $p\le n$: the regularization of the logarithmic interactions, the
diagonal corrections, and the completion of squares only involve sums over the points present. The
missing points change the energy bound by an amount linear in $m$, which is the cost of a fixed column.

For the parameters of Section~\ref{sec:twenty} this cost is controlled as follows. We use two pairs of
trial sequences, one for each case. For each case the cost of a fixed column is at most $20c_\kappa N$
in the exponent, where $c_\kappa$ is obtained by completing a square in the proportions $\alpha=m/n$ and
$\tau\le\alpha$ of fixed points of the two kinds. For the second case the quantity to be bounded is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:budget2}
 h_0+(2.6\log2-F+R-c_2)\alpha-2\log2\,\alpha^2+(\log2-R-0.75)\tau-\log2\,\tau^2,
\end{equation}
with $0\le\tau\le\alpha\le1$, where $F$ is the bound for the sum of the two fields, $R$ is the bound for the
field at the fixed points, and $h_0$ collects the constants. Here the fixed points are the quadrature
nodes, at which $|x|>g_0$; this is why the bound $R$ for the field at the fixed points is needed only on
$g_0\le|x|\le1$, where it is much better than on the whole interval. The expression \eqref{eq:budget2} is
negative on its domain when $c_2=4.29033$, and the analogous expression for the first case is negative
when $c_1=4.21$.

Combining this with Lemma~\ref{lem:quadrature}, a determinant $M_f$ with $m$ fixed columns contributes at
most $(540000N)^m e^{(20c_2+17.913)Nm}$ times the unperturbed bound; the factor $5^m$ comes from expanding
the row functions at the fixed points in the second case. Lemma~\ref{lem:expansion} then gives,
for $|\delta|\le e^{-\rho N}$ with $\rho=20c_2+17.913=259299/2500$, a factor $(1+O(N^2))^{20N}=e^{o(N^2)}$.
This proves Theorem~\ref{thm:real}, given the numerical bounds of the next two subsections; the
constant $\eta_\infty$ comes from
\[
 400\bigl(U_*-\tfrac12\log2-\mathsf A\bigr)=400U_*-\frac{8795}{12}-242\log2>\frac{5993}{3125},
\]
where $U_*$ is the energy bound at the real place given below, $\frac12\log2$ is the normalization of
the determinant, and $\mathsf A$ is the constant of Proposition~\ref{prop:denominators}.

\subsection{The trial sequences}\label{ssec:trials}
For the first case we use a trial sequence with $40$ Chebyshev coefficients and a finite number of
geometric terms, with an additional multiplier $\lambda=1.68493357$ for the constraint that defines the
case. For the second case we use trial sequences of the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:trial}
 u_k=c_k+\operatorname{Re}\sum_jr_jz_j^k\qquad(k\ge1;\ c_k=0\text{ for }k>K),
\end{equation}
with $K=640$ Chebyshev coefficients in $2^{-40}\Z$ and $56$ geometric terms, $22$ of them with complex
$z_j$ and complex $r_j$.

The geometric terms have a simple meaning. A term $(z,r)$ contributes
$-\frac12\operatorname{Re}\,r\log(1-2xz+z^2)$ to the potential $T(u,x)$. If $x_0+i\epsilon=\frac12(z+z^{-1})$
then $1-2xz+z^2=2z(x_0+i\epsilon-x)$, so the term is, up to a constant, the logarithmic potential of a point
charge of complex strength $r$ at $x_0+i\epsilon$ and its conjugate. The extremal measure of the energy
problem has a gap in its support around $x=0$, of radius about $0.1$, and its potential has a
logarithmic singularity at $x=0$ and square-root behaviour at the edges of the gap. A Chebyshev expansion
converges slowly near such points. A charge at a small distance $\epsilon$ from them reproduces the local
behaviour with one term. We place the complex charges at $x_0\in\{0,\pm0.03,\pm0.06,\pm0.09,\pm0.12,\pm0.16\}$
with $\epsilon\in\{10^{-2},3\cdot10^{-3}\}$; the numbers $z_j$ are rationals with twelve decimals. The norms
of the geometric terms have closed forms: two terms $(z_j,r_j)$, $(z_l,r_l)$ contribute
$-\frac12\operatorname{Re}\bigl(r_jr_l\log(1-z_jz_l)+r_j\bar r_l\log(1-z_j\bar z_l)\bigr)$.

Without the complex terms, the same $640$ coefficients and the $34$ real geometric terms give, in a
numerical optimization, no exponent below $728.5$.

\subsection{Verification of the bounds}\label{ssec:cells}
We must bound three functions of one variable from above on their whole domains: the field $X$ on
$[-1,1]$, the field $Y$ on $[0,1]$, and the field at the fixed points on $g_0\le|x|\le1$. With $x=\cos\theta$,
each is a constant plus a cosine series with $640$ terms plus finitely many terms
$\operatorname{Re}\,w\log(1-ae^{im\theta})$.

We cover each domain by intervals $[x_c-h,x_c+h]$ whose endpoints are multiples of $2^{-40}$, and on
each interval bound the function by its Taylor expansion at $x_c$. The polynomial part has degree $639$,
and a global bound for its fifth derivative would force very short intervals. Instead we compute the
derivatives of the polynomial part exactly at $x_c$ up to order $70$, and bound only the remainder beyond
order $70$ by a single global constant, which enters multiplied by $h^{70}/70!$. The logarithmic terms are
expanded to order $5$, with remainders bounded from the exact minimum of $|1-ae^{im\theta}|$ on the
interval. A few intervals at $x=0$ and $x=\pm1$ treat the singular terms separately. The covers have $598$,
$435$ and $366$ intervals. Each interval is checked by exact rational and integer arithmetic.

The resulting bounds are
\[
 \sup X\le-\frac{97745957}{10^8},\qquad \sup Y\le-\frac{156655368}{10^8},\qquad
 \sup_{g_0\le|x|\le1}R\le-\frac{5247033}{10^8},
\]
the norms satisfy $2\|u\|^2+\frac12\|v\|^2\le\frac{24149189}{31250000}$, and the first coefficient satisfies
$u_1\ge9/50$, which is used near $x=0$. These give the energy bound $U_*=141027507/62500000$ at the real place.
